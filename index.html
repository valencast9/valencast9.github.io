Angel Aerial Systems

Since May 2026, I have worked as an Electrical Engineering Intern at Angel Aerial Systems, where I contribute to the battery-system development for the Trio Scout, a rapidly deployable tricopter designed for flights of up to 100 minutes. My work spans the complete battery system, including pouch-cell assembly, high-current interconnects, mechanical integration, BMS electronics, embedded firmware, and system-level validation.

A major focus of my internship has been the redesign of the aircraft's Battery Management System (BMS) to improve reliability, serviceability, protection, and diagnostic capability. I helped translate field observations and operational requirements into hardware and firmware improvements, contributing to circuit design, component selection, PCB development, communication interfaces, fault handling, data logging, and hardware bring-up. I also worked extensively on debugging existing hardware and software, including leading the BMS-side root cause investigation of a battery-pack issue and using the findings to inform subsequent design improvements.

On the pack side, I helped transition the pouch-cell assembly to resistance-welded interconnects and designed the associated electrical and mechanical interfaces in Siemens NX. This required close collaboration with the mechanical team as we iterated on circuit-board geometry, cell-tab forming, component positioning, assembly sequencing, structural constraints, and service access within a highly space-constrained battery pack. The work taught me how strongly electrical, mechanical, thermal, and manufacturing decisions influence one another in a tightly integrated aerospace system.

I have also supported the development of test procedures and manufacturing processes used to bring up and validate the system. This has included checking electrical interfaces, verifying protection and monitoring behavior, evaluating assembly methods, and troubleshooting interactions between the battery pack, aircraft electronics, and embedded software.

Overall, this experience has given me hands-on responsibility for a safety-critical electrical system across the full engineering cycle, from investigating field issues and defining requirements to designing hardware, writing firmware, developing assembly processes, and validating the completed system.

Vertical Flight Systems (VFS) Purdue

At Purdue, I serve as the President of VFS Purdue for the International GoAERO VTOL Competition, where I lead a multidisciplinary team of approximately 30 engineering students in designing and building an autonomous, human-carrying aircraft. Our team earned the NASA University Innovation Award during Stage 1 of the competition.

During the initial design stage, I developed a preliminary aircraft-sizing tool in MATLAB and contributed to propulsion, avionics, energy-storage, and electrical architecture trade studies. This work helped connect the competition's mission requirements with practical constraints such as aircraft mass, endurance, power demand, controllability, and component availability given the current supply-chain hurdles in the drone space.

For Stage 2, our team designed, constructed, and autonomously flew a 40-percent-scale prototype. My technical responsibilities included propulsion and avionics sizing, electrical architecture, wiring and power-distribution design, component integration, and support during ground and flight testing. Working through the prototype's integration and test campaign gave me experience turning subsystem designs into a complete aircraft and troubleshooting the electrical, mechanical, and software interactions that only become visible at the system level.

For Stage 3, I am leading the team through the design and manufacturing of the full-scale aircraft. My role includes coordinating design reviews, procurement, manufacturing, testing, scheduling, safety planning, and technical decisions across the team's subgroups. Alongside these leadership responsibilities, I am focusing my technical work on the design of a roughly 100 V monitoring and balancing BMS for the aircraft's 14.4 kWh battery pack. This involves developing an architecture that can safely monitor a large series-connected battery while supporting reliable communication, fault detection, balancing, and integration with the rest of our avionics.

Purdue POWER Lab

From June through December 2025, I worked as an Undergraduate Researcher at the Purdue POWER Lab, where I supported the development and testing of a six-phase motor and drive prototype for UAV propulsion. My work combined motor-control simulation, embedded control development, PCB design, hardware bring-up, and experimental validation.

I began by developing a Simulink model of two synchronized three-phase Field-Oriented Control loops used to operate the six-phase machine,which allowed us to evaluate the proposed control architecture and validate the overall control behavior before moving forward with the physical system.

I also designed and routed a magnetic encoder PCB in Altium to provide precise rotor-position feedback to the motor controller. I then supported the integration of the encoder, control firmware, inverter hardware, and modified motor into the complete propulsion test setup. As testing progressed, I contributed to initial board bring-up, signal verification, control-loop tuning, current and speed-loop validation, and troubleshooting across the hardware and firmware interfaces.

The project culminated in testing on a propeller-based propulsion test stand, including operation of the complete six-phase system at peak test speeds and power. This work gave me hands-on experience with multiphase machines, power-electronic motor drives, advanced feedback control, real-time embedded systems, PCB development, and the practical challenges involved in taking a control design from simulation to a working electromechanical system.
