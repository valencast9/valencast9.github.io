Six-Phase Motor
Drive Research.

Developing a dual three-phase drive for UAV propulsion, from control architecture and the first modified stator to custom rotor-position hardware and sensored closed-loop operation.

I worked across the complete research cycle: defining the control approach, building the first motor and inverter prototypes, designing an encoder PCB, writing the software needed to configure it, and integrating position feedback into the physical drive.

The central challenge was to make two three-phase electrical systems behave as one six-phase machine. That meant the controller, power stage, modified stator, position sensor, and mechanical package all had to agree on the same rotor angle and timing.

01 / Control architecture

Starting with the control loop

Before modifying the motor, I worked through how the drive would be controlled. We began with a conventional three-phase motor on a dynamometer to establish a known hardware and measurement baseline, then expanded that structure into a proposed dual three-phase field-oriented control architecture.

The block diagram kept the familiar cascaded speed and current loops, Clarke and Park transforms, and space-vector modulation. Rotor position from the encoder closes the coordinate transforms, while synchronized commands feed two three-phase inverter groups. The diagram gave us a shared reference for deciding where sensing, protection, modulation, and firmware interfaces belonged before we committed to hardware.

02 / Six-phase hardware

Building the first electrical prototype

I helped create our first six-phase stator prototype by bringing out two three-phase winding groups and preparing the motor for independent drive connections. In parallel, we assembled the first power-stage prototype from a three-phase drive and a mirrored three-phase inverter, producing the six inverter legs needed to energize the modified machine.

The early setup was intentionally accessible. Terminal blocks exposed each winding connection, and bench instruments let us inspect signals while we worked through phase order, wiring, and synchronization. This stage turned the block diagram into a real electromechanical system whose interfaces could be measured and debugged.

03 / Encoder design

Adding rotor position for sensored control

Closed-loop FOC needed an accurate rotor angle at speed, so I selected the MPS MA732 magnetic encoder and designed a dedicated PCB around it in Altium. The device’s specified 0–60,000 RPM measurement range provided the speed baseline and headroom I wanted for a UAV motor, while its programmable SPI interface and hardware ABZ output fit the bring-up and real-time control workflow.

The board geometry was driven by the motor. I shaped the PCB around the stator’s available mounting points and placed the sensor directly over a diametrically magnetized rotor magnet. I designed for approximately 60 mT at the sensor, inside the MA732 datasheet’s 45–100 mT operating characterization range, then routed the configuration and incremental-output signals to a compact connector.

Turning the motor geometry into encoder settings

The MA732 is magnetic, so it does not have physical optical slits. I programmed the equivalent incremental resolution: 1,024 pulses per mechanical revolution on each AB channel. Counting all four quadrature edges produces 4,096 position counts per revolution.

With 21 pole pairs, one mechanical turn contains 21 electrical revolutions. Dividing 4,096 quadrature counts by 21 gives about 195 counts per electrical revolution. That is approximately 1.85 electrical degrees per count, fine enough to give the FOC loop a useful rotor-angle update throughout each electrical cycle.

04 / PCB bring-up

Programming the encoder over SPI

I wrote a C++ utility to configure and verify the MA732 over SPI. It first checked communication by reading the absolute angle, then programmed rotation direction, mechanical zero, the 1,024-pulse ABZ output, the fastest 64 µs filter window, and ABZ hysteresis. Every register write was read back so I could verify the configuration before connecting the encoder output to the motor controller.

SPI was used for setup and diagnostics; the encoder’s A, B, and index signals became the real-time interface to the dual three-phase control prototype. This separated one-time sensor configuration from the deterministic position edges used during closed-loop operation.

A practical lab detail: I repurposed a Raspberry Pi from an earlier drone project to flash and configure the board. I did not have a monitor available, so I connected the Pi to my TV. The board communicated, accepted its settings, and returned the expected readback on the first try.

05 / Integration & results

Closing the loop on the propulsion stand

With the stator, dual inverter, and encoder assembled, the rotor-position path was complete: the magnet produced the field, the MA732 measured angle, the programmed ABZ interface delivered incremental position, and the control firmware used that feedback for sensored operation.

The completed prototype progressed from bench-level signal checks to closed-loop testing with a propeller. That result validated the full development chain, including the control concept, six-phase hardware, custom sensing, configuration software, and physical integration, as one working electromechanical system.
